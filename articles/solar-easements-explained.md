The term "solar easement" is used to describe two meaningfully different legal instruments, and conflating them is one of the more common errors in early-stage land research. The sunlight access easement protects a solar installation from shading by neighboring structures. The transmission easement — sometimes called a gen-tie easement or right-of-way — gives a developer the right to run lines across neighboring land to reach a substation. They appear in land transactions for different reasons, and what to do about either one when you find it in a title search depends entirely on which type it is.

## What a Solar Easement Actually Is

In its original legal meaning, a solar easement is a recorded right that protects a solar energy system's access to unobstructed sunlight. The owner of the solar installation holds the easement against an adjacent or nearby property, which means the burdened property owner cannot build structures, plant trees, or allow vegetation growth that would shade the panels.

The legal definition is codified in statutes across most US states — the language typically specifies the easement's dimensions using horizontal and vertical angles or maximum height restrictions, and requires it to be recorded in the deed. Once recorded, the restriction runs with the land. If the burdened property sells, the new owner inherits the obligation. It doesn't disappear on transfer and it doesn't require the original parties to renegotiate.

California was among the first states to establish solar easement statutes in the 1970s. Today, the majority of states have enacted their own frameworks, though the specific provisions — what must be in the instrument, how it's recorded, whether compensation is required — vary considerably. A handful of states have no solar easement statute at all, which doesn't make the agreements unenforceable, but removes the statutory scaffolding that simplifies the process.

## How This Shows Up When You're Buying Land

For someone purchasing a rural parcel with the intention of leasing it for solar development, the sunlight access form of a solar easement rarely creates a problem. These easements exist most commonly in suburban or semi-rural areas where residential solar installations on neighboring properties are close enough for shading conflicts to arise. A 300-acre agricultural parcel in central Georgia has almost no risk of carrying this kind of encumbrance.

That said, any title review before closing should surface existing easements. A preliminary title search pulls documents recorded against the parcel, and a solar easement would appear there. If you're buying land adjacent to an established solar project, there's a higher-than-average chance the existing project owner has recorded easements or deed restrictions related to sunlight access that affect surrounding parcels.

The cleaner risk to check for in that scenario isn't the sunlight easement — it's the generation interconnection easement on the adjacent land, which is a different instrument.

## Generation Interconnection Easements: The More Common Issue in Land Purchases

When a utility-scale solar project is sited more than a short distance from a substation, the developer needs to build a dedicated transmission line — called a generation-tie or gen-tie line — to connect the project to the grid. That gen-tie doesn't always run across land the developer owns or leases. If the most direct route crosses neighboring parcels, the developer must acquire right-of-way easements from those landowners.

These are commonly called solar easements in deal documentation, though they're technically transmission or access easements. They grant the developer the right to run overhead or underground lines across a defined strip of the neighboring property, with a fixed width — typically 50 to 150 feet depending on the voltage of the line — along a described route. The landowner retains ownership and can continue using the land for farming or grazing in most cases, but cannot build structures within the easement corridor, plant tall vegetation, or obstruct maintenance access.

Gen-tie lines can run for several miles. Developers pay for these rights by negotiating with individual landowners along the route. Compensation is typically calculated on a per-linear-foot basis, reflecting the proportion of the parcel affected. The landowner retains use of the land outside the corridor — a meaningfully different situation from a [solar land lease](/blog/how-solar-land-lease-works), where surface use for the full project footprint transfers to the developer for the lease term.

If you purchase a parcel that already has a recorded gen-tie easement across it, you're acquiring the land with that restriction intact. The developer holds the right to maintain and access that corridor for the life of the agreement, which is typically tied to the operational term of the solar project it serves — 25 to 40 years.

## What to Check Before Making an Offer

For any parcel you're evaluating for solar land investment, the title review process should specifically look for:

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Recorded easements against the parcel | Transmission and access easements restrict portions of the land and survive sale |
| Sunlight access easements burdening the parcel | Less common in rural areas; more likely near established residential solar |
| Easements held by the parcel against neighboring land | Potentially valuable — existing rights-of-way can signal prior developer interest |
| Conservation easements | Permanently restrict use for solar development; a title-level disqualifier |
| Utility easements | Overhead lines often carry blanket restrictions that affect buildable area |

A permanent conservation easement — granted to a land trust or government entity — runs with the land indefinitely and typically prohibits any development that would alter the property's natural character. Solar development almost always fails that test. No developer will proceed past due diligence on a conserved parcel, which makes it effectively worthless for leasing purposes. These easements show up in a title search, but the mechanism is different from solar easements — they're held by third-party conservation organizations, not neighboring landowners.

Utility easements are also worth reading carefully. An existing overhead transmission line crossing the parcel may seem like an asset — grid proximity matters significantly for [what makes land viable for a solar farm](/blog/what-makes-land-viable-solar-farm) — but the easement the utility holds often includes a blanket restriction on any construction within a defined corridor below the lines. Depending on how the project is sited, this can reduce the usable acreage more than the physical footprint of the lines suggests.

## How Developers Use Easements in a Lease Transaction

When a developer executes a solar lease, the lease document itself typically includes easement rights beyond the main project footprint. These cover access roads to reach the project from a public road, interconnection corridors where transmission infrastructure needs to cross adjacent land, and sometimes temporary construction easements on land adjacent to the main site.

A lease that looks clean on the base acreage can quietly carry significant adjacent easement obligations, particularly if the interconnection route hasn't been fully engineered when the lease is signed. Before signing a solar option agreement, it's worth understanding which easements the developer intends to acquire from neighboring landowners, and whether any of those neighbors could complicate or delay the interconnection route — since a protracted easement negotiation can stall a project after your option is already locked.
